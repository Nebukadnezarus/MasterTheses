\chapter{Control Architecture}\label{chapter:control-architecture}
\label{chapter:control}

This chapter presents the control architecture used to stabilize and command the
aerodynamically augmented quadrotor. The controller is organized into three
conceptually distinct layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item an \textbf{outer-loop translational controller} that regulates position
    and generates a commanded specific force,
    \item a \textbf{geometric attitude controller} on $SO(3)$ that converts the
    commanded force into a desired orientation and computes a desired angular
    acceleration, and
    \item an \textbf{INDI inner loop} that realizes this angular acceleration
    through incremental torque updates while rejecting unmodeled aerodynamic
    disturbances.
\end{enumerate}

This hierarchical decomposition separates slow translational dynamics from fast
rotational dynamics and ensures robustness to aerodynamic forces produced by
the fixed wings. No aerodynamic model enters any part of the closed-loop
control law; aerodynamic lift and drag appear only as disturbances and are
rejected by the incremental structure of INDI.

\section{Overview of the Cascaded Architecture}

The controller follows the three-layer structure introduced above:
an outer-loop translational controller, a geometric attitude controller, and an INDI inner loop.
The outer loop provides a commanded specific
force $a_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ that aligns the vehicle's thrust vector with the
desired translational acceleration. This force is mapped to a desired attitude
$R_d \in SO(3)$, whose tracking is delegated to the geometric attitude
controller. The geometric controller generates a desired angular acceleration
$\alpha_d$ based on the attitude error. Finally, the INDI inner loop computes
the necessary incremental changes in motor thrusts to realize $\alpha_d$ and
the commanded collective thrust.

The complete set of controller gains and model parameters used in the cascaded
architecture is summarized in Table~\ref{tab:control-parameters}.
All variables and mathematical symbols used in this chapter are summarized in Table~\ref{tab:notation-control}.

\section{Outer-Loop Translational Control}
\label{sec:outer-loop}

The outer loop regulates position and velocity in $\mathbb{R}^3$. Its purpose is
to generate a \emph{commanded specific force} --- an acceleration in the world
frame that the attitude controller should realize through appropriate
orientation of the thrust vector.

\subsection{Commanded Translational Acceleration}

Given desired position $p_d$, velocity $v_d$, feedforward acceleration $a_d$,
and yaw $\psi_d$, the commanded acceleration is computed using a linear PD
controller:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{cmd}} =
K_p(p_d - p) + K_d(v_d - v) + a_d - g.
\label{eq:outer-pd}
\end{equation}

The formulation is intentionally simple. Because aerodynamic forces are handled
as disturbances at the lower levels, the translational controller does not rely
on an aerodynamic model.

\subsection{Acceleration Mismatch Disturbance Estimate}

During horizontal flight, the wings generate significant aerodynamic lift and
drag. These forces appear in the translational dynamics as unmodeled
disturbances. A lightweight acceleration-mismatch observer improves outer-loop
performance without compromising robustness.

Let $\hat{a}$ be the filtered specific force measured by the IMU and
$T_f$ the filtered estimate of the current total thrust produced by the rotors.
The aerodynamic disturbance in the world frame is estimated as
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{aero}} = 
R\left(\frac{T_f}{m} e_3 - \hat{a}\right),
\end{equation}
where $R$ is the current attitude. This quantity captures discrepancies between
the expected thrust-induced acceleration and the actual measured acceleration,
which arise primarily from aerodynamic forces.

When the vehicle is at sufficient altitude and speed, this term improves
tracking performance by compensating for drag and unmodeled lift:
\begin{equation}
a_{\mathrm{cmd}} \gets a_{\mathrm{cmd}} + a_{\mathrm{aero}}.
\end{equation}

\subsection{Desired Thrust}

The outer loop computes the commanded collective thrust:
\begin{equation}
T_{\mathrm{cmd}} = m\, a_{\mathrm{cmd}}^\top R e_3,
\end{equation}
where $e_3 = [0\;0\;1]^\top$. This scalar is passed to the inner loop.

\section{Geometric Attitude Control on \texorpdfstring{$SO(3)$}{SO(3)}}
\label{sec:geometric-attitude}

The geometric attitude controller converts the commanded specific force
$a_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ into a desired attitude and generates a desired angular
acceleration that the INDI loop must realize.

\subsection{Desired Attitude Construction}

To realize $a_{\mathrm{cmd}}$, the thrust vector must be aligned with
$-z_B$. The desired body $z$-axis is therefore
\begin{equation}
z_B = \frac{a_{\mathrm{cmd}}}{\|a_{\mathrm{cmd}}\|}.
\end{equation}

A desired horizontal heading is obtained from $\psi_d$ or from a forward
velocity direction when appropriate. This defines the desired $x_B$, and the
desired $y_B$ follows from orthogonality:
\[
R_d = \big[\,x_B\;\; y_B\;\; z_B\,\big].
\]

This construction ensures that the translational controller dictates the thrust
vector orientation.

\subsection{Geometric Attitude Error}

Attitude error is defined directly on the manifold $SO(3)$ using the canonical
geometric error~\cite{Lee2010}:
\begin{equation}
e_R = 
\frac{1}{2}\left(R_d^\top R - R^\top R_d\right)^\vee.
\label{eq:geometric-error}
\end{equation}

This error represents the minimal rotation that aligns $R$ with $R_d$ and avoids
the singularities of Euler-angle parameterizations. For numerical robustness, an equivalent quaternion-based implementation of~\eqref{eq:geometric-error} is used, but the underlying control law is defined on $SO(3)$.

\subsection{Desired Angular Rate and Angular Acceleration}

A nonlinear mapping from the attitude error to a desired angular rate yields
\[
\omega_d = f(e_R),
\]
where $f$ preserves yaw decoupling and prioritizes pitch and roll tracking.

Angular acceleration is then computed using proportional feedback on the rate
error:
\begin{equation}
\alpha_d = \omega_d + K_\omega(\omega_d - \omega).
\label{eq:alpha-des}
\end{equation}

Although the controller computes rates and accelerations internally, the
regulated variable is \emph{attitude}. The geometric layer provides
$\alpha_d$ to the INDI inner loop.

\section{INDI Inner-Loop Attitude Control}
\label{sec:indi}

Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) is used to track the desired
angular acceleration $\alpha_d$ and reject all unmodeled disturbances,
including those caused by aerodynamic forces from the wings. Unlike classical
dynamic inversion, INDI does not rely on an accurate model of the rotational
dynamics. Instead, it uses motor-speed feedback and filtered gyro acceleration
to compute incremental changes to the control input.

\subsection{Thrust and Torque Estimation}

Rotor thrusts are estimated using a static lookup table obtained from
test-stand measurements. The table maps motor command inputs to measured
thrust values for multiple battery voltages. During flight, the current
voltage is used to interpolate between table slices, yielding an accurate
estimate of the instantaneous rotor thrusts $T_i$.
These thrusts are mapped through the allocation matrix $G$ to produce the
estimated torque:
\[
\tau_f = (G T_f)_{1:3}.
\]
This approach eliminates dependence on a parametric thrust model and provides
accurate thrust estimates even under voltage sag, temperature changes, or
motor heating.

\subsection{Incremental Torque Update}

The INDI update law is:
\begin{equation}
\tau =
\tau_f + J(\alpha_d - \dot\omega_f),
\label{eq:indi-update}
\end{equation}
where $\dot{\omega}_f$ is the filtered angular acceleration measured by the IMU.
This expression cancels the measured angular acceleration and replaces it with
the desired one. Consequently, any slowly varying disturbance torque ---
including aerodynamic moments --- is automatically rejected.

\subsection{Allocation to Rotor Thrusts}

The commanded torque is combined with the commanded collective thrust:
\[
\mu = [T_{\mathrm{cmd}},\, \tau]^\top,
\]
and mapped to rotor thrusts using the allocation matrix:
\[
T = G^{-1}\mu.
\]

This yields the four motor thrust commands.

\section{Stability Discussion}

The controller is stable under standard assumptions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item The geometric attitude error~\eqref{eq:geometric-error} admits a
    Lyapunov function on $SO(3)$.
    \item The outer-loop linear controller operates on slower translational
    dynamics, allowing separation of time scales.
    \item INDI accurately realizes $\alpha_d$ as long as motor dynamics are fast
    relative to attitude dynamics.
    \item Aerodynamic forces and torques appear only as slowly varying
    disturbances and are canceled by the incremental term
    $(\alpha_d - \dot\omega_f)$.
\end{itemize}

\section{Summary}

The control architecture is a clean cascade: a linear translational controller
commands a specific force, a geometric attitude controller generates a desired
angular acceleration, and an INDI inner loop realizes this acceleration while
rejecting aerodynamic disturbances. This architecture leverages the aerodynamic
benefits of fixed wings without requiring an aerodynamic model, enabling
efficient forward flight with disturbance robustness.
