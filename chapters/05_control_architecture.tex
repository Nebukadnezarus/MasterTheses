\chapter{Control Architecture}\label{chapter:control-architecture}

This chapter describes the control structure that stabilizes the X-wing quadrotor
across hover and forward flight. The architecture follows a cascaded multirotor
design with three main layers:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item a \emph{conventional PD-type translational outer loop} that generates a commanded
          specific force,
    \item a \emph{geometric attitude and rate controller} that converts this
          force into a desired attitude and angular-rate command, and
    \item an \emph{Incremental Nonlinear Dynamic Inversion (INDI) torque controller}
          that tracks the desired angular acceleration using measured angular acceleration
          and motor-speed feedback.
\end{enumerate}

No aerodynamic model enters any part of the closed-loop control law. The additional
forces and moments generated by the fixed wings appear only through their influence
on measured accelerations and angular rates. These effects are rejected implicitly
by the incremental INDI update and by a small measurement-based feedforward term
in the outer loop.

A schematic overview is shown in Fig.~\ref{fig:control-structure}. All symbols and
gains specific to the controller are summarized in
Table~\ref{tab:control-notation-parameters}, and the numerical values of the
control gains are provided in Table~\ref{tab:control-gains}.

% =====================================================================
\section{Overview of the Cascaded Architecture}
% =====================================================================

The controller operates on the state $(p, v, R, \omega)$ introduced in
Chapter~\ref{chapter:dynamics-model}, where $p \in \mathbb{R}^3$ and $v \in \mathbb{R}^3$
denote position and velocity in the world frame, $R \in SO(3)$ is the attitude
(rotation matrix from body to world), and $\omega \in \mathbb{R}^3$ is the body
angular velocity.

At each control step, the following sequence is executed:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item The position and velocity errors $(e_p, e_v)$ are computed and a
          commanded specific force $a_d$ is generated by a linear PD law. A
          small correction based on the difference between thrust-induced and
          measured specific force is added at higher altitude.
    \item The commanded force is mapped to a desired thrust magnitude $T_d$ and
          a desired attitude $R_d$ (implemented as a quaternion $q_{\mathrm{cmd}}$).
    \item A geometric, tilt-prioritized attitude controller computes a desired
          angular rate $\omega_d$ from the attitude error $R^\top R_d$.
    \item A rate-feedback law combines $\omega_d$ and measured angular rates to
          form a desired angular acceleration $\alpha_d$.
    \item The INDI inner loop computes an updated torque command by comparing
          $\alpha_d$ to the measured angular acceleration and incrementing the
          current torque estimate accordingly.
    \item Finally, thrust and torque commands are mapped to individual rotor
          thrusts via the allocation matrix that includes the full rotor geometry.
\end{enumerate}

This decomposition separates slow translational dynamics from fast rotational
dynamics and ensures that all unmodeled aerodynamic forces are handled at the
innermost level, where high-rate acceleration measurements are available.

% =====================================================================
\section{Outer-Loop Translational Control}
% =====================================================================

Let $p_d(t)$ and $v_d(t)$ denote the desired position and velocity trajectories.
The position and velocity errors are
\begin{equation}
    e_p = p_d - p, \qquad
    e_v = v_d - v.
\end{equation}
In the implementation, both errors are clipped component-wise to bounded values
to avoid excessively large commands during transient maneuvers:
\begin{equation}
    e_p \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(e_p, \|e_p\|_\infty \leq e_{p,\max}), \qquad
    e_v \leftarrow \mathrm{clip}(e_v, \|e_v\|_\infty \leq e_{v,\max}).
\end{equation}

A linear PD controller generates a commanded specific force $a_{\mathrm{cmd}}$:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\mathrm{cmd}} =
      K_p e_p
    + K_v e_v
    + a_d
    - g,
\end{equation}
where $K_p, K_v \in \mathbb{R}^{3 \times 3}$ are diagonal gain matrices, $a_d$ is
the feedforward desired acceleration from the trajectory generator, and
$g = [0, 0, g]^\top$ is the gravity vector in the world frame.

\paragraph{Aerodynamic correction from measurements.}
To improve behavior in forward flight without introducing an explicit aerodynamic
model, an additional correction term is computed from IMU and motor-speed
measurements. The instantaneous thrust of rotor $i$ is estimated from the measured
squared motor speed $\omega_i^2$ via the static thrust map
\begin{equation}
    f_i \approx k_f \,\omega_i^2 + b_f,
\end{equation}
with coefficients identified on the thrust stand (see
Section~\ref{sec:thrust-identification}). Low-pass filtering is applied to
$\omega_i^2$ to reduce noise.

Let $f_T = \sum_i f_i$ denote the total thrust magnitude and
$a_{\mathrm{IMU}}$ the low-pass filtered specific force measured by the IMU in the
body frame. The acceleration that would result from thrust alone is
\begin{equation}
    a_{\mathrm{thrust}} = R^\top \left(\frac{f_T}{m} e_3\right),
\end{equation}
where $e_3 = [0, 0, 1]^\top$ is the body $z$-axis. The difference
\begin{equation}
    a_{\mathrm{aero}} = R \left(a_{\mathrm{thrust}} - a_{\mathrm{IMU}}\right)
\end{equation}
provides a measurement-based estimate of the net aerodynamic acceleration in the
world frame. For altitudes above a small threshold (0.5\,m in the implementation),
this term is added to the commanded acceleration:
\begin{equation}
    a_{\mathrm{cmd}} \leftarrow a_{\mathrm{cmd}} + a_{\mathrm{aero}}, \qquad
    \text{if } p_z > 0.5\,\mathrm{m}.
\end{equation}
This correction depends only on measured accelerations and motor speeds; it does
not rely on any aerodynamic coefficients and remains negligible in hover and
low-speed flight.

% =====================================================================
\section{Force-to-Attitude Mapping}
% =====================================================================

The commanded specific force $a_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ defines the desired total force in
the world frame:
\begin{equation}
    f_d = m\,a_{\mathrm{cmd}}.
\end{equation}
The direction of the desired body $z$-axis is chosen to align with this force:
\begin{equation}
    b_{3,d} = \frac{f_d}{\|f_d\|}.
\end{equation}

Rather than using the full force magnitude $T_d = \|f_d\|$ as in classical
geometric controllers, the thrust command is computed by projecting the desired
acceleration onto the current body $z$-axis:
\begin{equation}
    T_d = m\,a_{\mathrm{cmd}}^\top (R e_3).
\end{equation}
This conservative formulation ensures that thrust is applied only in the
direction the vehicle is currently facing. It prevents acceleration in
unintended directions when attitude tracking lags behind and reduces
the coupling between aggressive translational and rotational maneuvers.

The desired yaw angle $\psi_d$ defines the heading. A reference heading vector
\begin{equation}
    b_{1,\psi} =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        \cos \psi_d \\[2pt]
        \sin \psi_d \\[2pt]
        0
    \end{bmatrix}
\end{equation}
is constructed in the world frame. Using the standard geometric construction,
the desired body-frame axes are
\begin{align}
    b_{2,d} &= \frac{b_{3,d} \times b_{1,\psi}}{\|b_{3,d} \times b_{1,\psi}\|},\\
    b_{1,d} &= b_{2,d} \times b_{3,d}.
\end{align}
The desired attitude is then
\begin{equation}
    R_d = \begin{bmatrix} b_{1,d} & b_{2,d} & b_{3,d} \end{bmatrix} \in SO(3),
\end{equation}
which is implemented as a quaternion $q_{\mathrm{cmd}}$.

In practice, the implementation blends this construction with a velocity-aligned
heading to encourage coordinated flight: when the horizontal speed is
sufficiently large, the desired $x$-axis is aligned with the velocity vector
projected onto the horizontal plane. Singular cases where $b_{3,d}$ aligns with
this direction are handled explicitly to avoid numerical issues and abrupt yaw
flips.

\section{Geometric Attitude and Rate Control}
% =====================================================================

The attitude controller tracks $R_d$ using a geometric, tilt-prioritized
formulation. The core idea is to compute a desired angular rate $\omega_d$ that
reduces the attitude error while weighting tilt errors more strongly than yaw
errors.

Let $q$ and $q_{\mathrm{des}}$ denote the current and desired attitudes as
quaternions, and define the attitude error
\begin{equation}
    q_e = q^{-1} q_{\mathrm{des}}.
\end{equation}
Following the approach of F\"ohn et al.~\cite{Foehn2020}, a tilt-prioritized
matrix $T_{\mathrm{att}} = \mathrm{diag}(k_{R,xy}, k_{R,xy}, k_{R,z})$ is used to
weight roll/pitch versus yaw errors. A transformed error vector $e_{\mathrm{att}}$
is computed from $q_e$ and mapped to a rate command
\begin{equation}
    \omega_d = T_{\mathrm{att}}\,\Phi(q_e),
\end{equation}
where $\Phi(\cdot)$ is a smooth function of the quaternion error that penalizes
tilt deviations more strongly than yaw. This yields the desired body angular
rate $\omega_d$.

To obtain a desired angular acceleration, a rate-feedback law is applied:
\begin{equation}
    \alpha_d = \omega_d + K_\omega (\omega_{\mathrm{ref}} - \omega),
\end{equation}
where $K_\omega = \mathrm{diag}(k_{\omega,x}, k_{\omega,y}, k_{\omega,z})$ is a
diagonal rate-gain matrix and $\omega_{\mathrm{ref}}$ is an optional feedforward
rate command from the trajectory generator (typically zero in the experiments).
The resulting $\alpha_d$ is interpreted as the desired angular acceleration for
the attitude subsystem and is passed to the INDI loop as a virtual control input.

In summary, the geometric block:
\begin{itemize}
    \item computes a desired attitude $R_d$ (or $q_{\mathrm{des}}$) from the
          commanded force and yaw,
    \item maps the attitude error to a desired angular rate $\omega_d$ using
          tilt-prioritized geometric control, and
    \item augments $\omega_d$ with rate feedback to form a desired angular
          acceleration $\alpha_d$.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Philosophical distinction: geometric vs. INDI.}
The geometric attitude and rate controller is fundamentally \emph{model-based}: it computes a desired angular acceleration $\alpha_d$ using the known inertia matrix $J$ and assumes that commanding this acceleration will drive the attitude error to zero. In contrast, the INDI inner loop described in the next section is \emph{model-free}: it does not rely on $J$ or any other dynamic model but instead uses measured angular acceleration and motor-speed feedback to incrementally adjust the torque command. This separation of roles is deliberate. The geometric layer provides a stable, well-understood reference trajectory for angular acceleration, while INDI ensures that this trajectory is tracked robustly in the presence of unmodeled disturbances---including aerodynamic moments from the fixed wings. The two layers complement each other: geometric control establishes what the vehicle should do, and INDI ensures it actually does so despite model uncertainties.

\section{INDI Inner-Loop Torque Control}
% =====================================================================

The INDI inner loop computes the torque command so that the measured angular
acceleration tracks $\alpha_d$ despite unmodeled aerodynamic moments and other
disturbances. The starting point is the rotational dynamics
\begin{equation}
    J \dot{\omega} = \tau + \tau_{\mathrm{aero}} + \tau_{\mathrm{dist}},
\end{equation}
where $J$ is the inertia matrix, $\tau$ the control torque, and
$\tau_{\mathrm{aero}} + \tau_{\mathrm{dist}}$ represent aerodynamic and other
disturbances.

Let $\omega_{\mathrm{meas}}$ denote the gyroscope measurement, filtered by a
low-pass filter, and let
\begin{equation}
    \dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{meas}} \approx
    \frac{\omega_{\mathrm{meas}}[k] - \omega_{\mathrm{meas}}[k-1]}{\Delta t}
\end{equation}
be the corresponding finite-difference angular acceleration, further smoothed
by the same filter. The current rotor thrusts $f_i$ are again estimated from the
filtered squared motor speeds using the static thrust map, and the corresponding
torque is reconstructed via the allocation matrix $G$ (see below), yielding an
estimate $\tau_f$ of the torque currently produced by the rotors.

The INDI update then computes a new torque command $\tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}$ as
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{cmd}} =
      \tau_f
    + J \bigl( \alpha_d - \dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{meas}} \bigr).
\end{equation}
This expression can be interpreted as follows: if the measured angular
acceleration is smaller than commanded, the torque is incremented in the
direction of $J (\alpha_d - \dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{meas}})$; if it is larger, the
torque is reduced. Slowly varying disturbances such as aerodynamic moments enter
both the previous torque $\tau_f$ and the measured acceleration
$\dot{\omega}_{\mathrm{meas}}$ and therefore cancel in the increment. As a
result, the loop tracks $\alpha_d$ without requiring any explicit model of
$\tau_{\mathrm{aero}}$.

For comparison and as a fallback, a nonlinear dynamic inversion (NDI) torque
command
\begin{equation}
    \tau_{\mathrm{NDI}} = J \alpha_d + \omega \times (J \omega)
\end{equation}
is also computed. In the implementation, the yaw component of the torque command
is taken from $\tau_{\mathrm{NDI}}$ to suppress yaw oscillations, and the entire
NDI torque $\tau_{\mathrm{NDI}}$ is used whenever the vehicle is close to the
ground or the INDI mode is disabled. This does not change the conceptual role of
INDI as the primary disturbance-rejection mechanism during normal flight.

% =====================================================================
\section{Motor Allocation}
% =====================================================================

The final step is to map the total thrust and torque command to individual rotor
thrusts. Let
\begin{equation}
    \mu =
    \begin{bmatrix}
        T_{\mathrm{cmd}} \\
        \tau_{\mathrm{cmd}}
    \end{bmatrix}
    \in \mathbb{R}^4
\end{equation}
denote the commanded collective thrust and body torque. The allocation matrix
$G \in \mathbb{R}^{4 \times 4}$ encodes the effect of each rotor thrust on total
thrust and torque, including the arm geometry, motor tilt, and reaction
torques:
\begin{equation}
    \mu = G f, \qquad f = [f_1, f_2, f_3, f_4]^\top.
\end{equation}
Since the four rotors provide four independent control inputs, $G$ is square
and invertible, and the commanded rotor thrusts are
\begin{equation}
    f = G^{-1} \mu.
\end{equation}
The resulting thrusts are finally converted to motor-speed setpoints using the
inverse static thrust map identified on the test stand. Saturation limits on
individual rotor thrusts and on the total thrust are enforced at this stage to
respect actuator constraints.

\section{Summary}
% =====================================================================

The control architecture consists of:
\begin{itemize}
    \item a conventional PD-type translational outer loop that produces a commanded specific
          force from position and velocity errors,
    \item a force-to-attitude mapping that defines a desired attitude and thrust
          magnitude consistent with this force,
    \item a geometric, tilt-prioritized attitude and rate controller that
          generates a desired angular acceleration $\alpha_d$,
    \item an INDI-based torque controller that adjusts the torque command based on measured
          angular acceleration and motor-speed feedback, and
    \item a motor allocation step that maps thrust and torque to individual
          rotor thrusts using the full rotor geometry and tilt.
\end{itemize}

Aerodynamic forces and moments generated by the fixed wings are not modeled
explicitly. Instead, they are handled implicitly by the combination of
measurement-based acceleration correction in the outer loop and the incremental
structure of the INDI torque update. This allows the X-wing quadrotor to
maintain accurate tracking from hover to forward flight without any aerodynamic
parameter identification or gain scheduling.
